## Credibility is the whole point

A campaign site making a structural argument about English football has
exactly one asset that matters more than the argument itself: whether
readers can trust how it handles evidence. It would be easy to write a
persuasive case for structured adaptability using confident-sounding
statistics, invented study findings, or a quotation attributed to an FA
document nobody checked. It would also be dishonest, and it would undermine
the argument the moment a single reader checked a source and found it did
not say what we claimed.

So this site works the other way round. Every factual claim on it is either
backed by a labelled source, or framed explicitly as an analytical reading
rather than a fact. That discipline is more important to us than any
individual claim landing well.

## The source model

Every citation on this site resolves to an entry in a structured
bibliography. Each entry records what kind of source it is (official
federation material, peer-reviewed academic research, data journalism,
credible sports journalism, a book, an interview, or a historical account),
along with its publication date, the date we accessed it, which countries
and eras it relates to, and exactly what claim it is being used to support.

## Separating evidence from interpretation

Two different kinds of statement appear throughout this site, and we try
never to let them blur together. The first kind is a **factual claim**:
something that happened, something an official body published, something a
dataset recorded. The second is an **interpretation**: our reading of what
a pattern of facts might mean, offered because we think it is a reasonable
account, not because it has been proven.

Interpretations are visually marked wherever they appear, using
`<Callout tone="interpretation">`, and genuinely disputed readings are marked
`tone="contested"` instead. If a sentence on this site makes an analytical
claim without either a citation or an interpretation label, that is an
error in our own discipline. We mean it when we invite readers to tell us
where we have missed one.

## Why tactical causation resists proof

Nobody can run a controlled experiment on international football. There is
no version of Euro 2024 where England fields an identical squad under a
different national coaching framework so the two outcomes can be compared.
Every claim this site makes about what causes collective tactical fluency
(including its central argument) rests on inference from imperfect evidence:
team-cognition and coordination research conducted mostly outside elite
senior football <Cite id="shared-mental-models-research" />

<Cite id="team-coordination-research" />, observation of match patterns, and
comparison across national systems that differ in dozens of ways at once. We try
to be explicit about that limit rather than borrow the authority of "evidence"
for claims that are really structural reasoning.

<Callout tone="note" title="Tournament wins are not proof of a system">
  A team can win a major tournament playing a flawed system, and lose one
  playing an excellent system, because a single-elimination competition is a
  small, high-variance sample: a handful of matches, refereeing decisions,
  injuries and moments of individual brilliance that could easily have gone the
  other way. Post-tournament technical reports document what happened on the
  pitch in useful detail <Cite id="tournament-possession-data" />, but what
  happened is not the same as proof that it was the correct approach. We try
  never to argue "team X won, therefore approach X was validated," and we ask
  readers to be suspicious of anyone who does.
</Callout>

## How to treat our claims

Read this site the way we would want any serious source read: check the
citation, note whether it is marked verified or placeholder, and treat
anything inside an interpretation callout as an argument still open to
challenge. Where we discuss preparation-time constraints on
international coaching <Cite id="international-preparation-time" />, treat
the underlying figures as needing confirmation until the source entry says
otherwise. Where we lean on research from outside football (team cognition,
skill transfer, organisational design), treat it explicitly as analogy,
because that is what it is.

## An open invitation

If you find a citation that does not support the claim attached to it, a
placeholder source that has since been verified elsewhere, or an
interpretation we have presented without the label it needs, tell us. The
contact details are on the `/about/` page. A site built on evidentiary
discipline should welcome correction more than it welcomes agreement.
