Evidence

5-minute read

Credibility is the whole point

A campaign site making a structural argument about English football has exactly one asset that matters more than the argument itself: whether readers can trust how it handles evidence. It would be easy to write a persuasive case for structured adaptability using confident-sounding statistics, invented study findings, or a quotation attributed to an FA document nobody checked. It would also be dishonest, and it would undermine the argument the moment a single reader checked a source and found it did not say what we claimed.

So this site works the other way round. Every factual claim on it is either backed by a labelled source, or framed explicitly as an analytical reading rather than a fact. That discipline is more important to us than any individual claim landing well.

The source model

Every citation on this site resolves to an entry in a structured bibliography. Each entry records what kind of source it is (official federation material, peer-reviewed academic research, data journalism, credible sports journalism, a book, an interview, or a historical account), along with its publication date, the date we accessed it, which countries and eras it relates to, and exactly what claim it is being used to support.

No source in our bibliography is yet fully verified. A research pass has confirmed the exact citation details of just over half of them against multiple independent records (those are marked "corroborated"), but nobody has yet retrieved and read each underlying document against the claim it supports. Treat every citation on this site as "here is what we have found and still need to check," not "this has been checked."

We built this site's argument first and its bibliography second, and we are saying so plainly rather than disguising the order. For each source, we have written a specific note describing exactly what a researcher needs to find, verify or cross-check before the claim attached to it can be treated as confirmed: an exact date, a specific figure, the actual wording of an official document, or the existence of a genuine scholarly consensus rather than a plausible-sounding assumption. Until that verification work is done, every source carries a visible status, shown wherever it is cited: "corroborated" where the citation's identity has been confirmed against independent records but its text remains unread, "placeholder" where only a candidate has been located.

This is a structural decision, made deliberately: we would rather publish an argument with honestly labelled unverified sources than publish one with confidently unlabelled invented ones. Readers, including anyone at the FA, should treat every number, date and quotation-adjacent claim on this site as provisional until the bibliography says otherwise.

The verification status field exists in the underlying data model specifically so this distinction cannot be silently dropped as the site grows: every source is typed as verified, corroborated or placeholder; the placeholder state is the default we started from, and verified is reserved for sources that have actually been retrieved and read, a bar nothing has yet met. See docs/sources-methodology for how a source moves from placeholder to verified, and docs/RESEARCH_STANDARDS.md for the rules governing the comparative national case studies specifically.

Separating evidence from interpretation

Two different kinds of statement appear throughout this site, and we try never to let them blur together. The first kind is a factual claim: something that happened, something an official body published, something a dataset recorded. The second is an interpretation: our reading of what a pattern of facts might mean, offered because we think it is a reasonable account, not because it has been proven.

Interpretations are visually marked wherever they appear, using <Callout tone="interpretation">, and genuinely disputed readings are marked tone="contested" instead. If a sentence on this site makes an analytical claim without either a citation or an interpretation label, that is an error in our own discipline. We mean it when we invite readers to tell us where we have missed one.

Why tactical causation resists proof

Nobody can run a controlled experiment on international football. There is no version of Euro 2024 where England fields an identical squad under a different national coaching framework so the two outcomes can be compared. Every claim this site makes about what causes collective tactical fluency (including its central argument) rests on inference from imperfect evidence: team-cognition and coordination research conducted mostly outside elite senior football

, observation of match patterns, and comparison across national systems that differ in dozens of ways at once. We try to be explicit about that limit rather than borrow the authority of "evidence" for claims that are really structural reasoning.

Note· Tournament wins are not proof of a system

A team can win a major tournament playing a flawed system, and lose one playing an excellent system, because a single-elimination competition is a small, high-variance sample: a handful of matches, refereeing decisions, injuries and moments of individual brilliance that could easily have gone the other way. Post-tournament technical reports document what happened on the pitch in useful detail , but what happened is not the same as proof that it was the correct approach. We try never to argue "team X won, therefore approach X was validated," and we ask readers to be suspicious of anyone who does.

How to treat our claims

Read this site the way we would want any serious source read: check the citation, note whether it is marked verified or placeholder, and treat anything inside an interpretation callout as an argument still open to challenge. Where we discuss preparation-time constraints on international coaching , treat the underlying figures as needing confirmation until the source entry says otherwise. Where we lean on research from outside football (team cognition, skill transfer, organisational design), treat it explicitly as analogy, because that is what it is.

An open invitation

If you find a citation that does not support the claim attached to it, a placeholder source that has since been verified elsewhere, or an interpretation we have presented without the label it needs, tell us. The contact details are on the /about/ page. A site built on evidentiary discipline should welcome correction more than it welcomes agreement.
